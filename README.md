
**Is the World Continuous or Discrete? The following is my personal view, not a textbook position. Readers are encouraged to read critically.**

Zou, Zhi Kai 邹志凯

Email:zhiyan.zou@foxmail.com Wuhan; China

https://orcid.org/0009-0000-4279-1064

A personal, critical reflection on whether reality is continuous or discrete, arguing that continuity is a useful mathematical idealization but lacks physical instantiation, while discreteness better aligns with observation and computation.

1. In the real world, we have not found any physical object that satisfies the mathematical definition of continuity—that is, there is no known instance in reality of the kind of infinite approximability that continuity entails.

2. The continuous mathematical framework based on the completeness of real numbers has been highly successful and effective in engineering and physical computations most of the time, providing a very powerful tool for human engineering design and calculation. This actually shows that even for discrete objects, as long as certain conditions are met, continuous mathematics can be used for computation in an efficient, effective, and practical way.

3. In the computer age, because computers are binary at their core and programming languages rely on discrete objects, one simple example is numerical relativity—based on differential geometry—whose computational kernels are actually discretized approximations. Yet the results from numerical relativity already closely match those from continuous differential geometry. This in turn suggests that there is no insurmountable barrier between discrete and continuous mathematics when it comes to computation.

4. In my view, the philosophy of discrete mathematics is more about trying to make mathematics a faithful mapping of reality. There is also the practical advantage of programmability.

5. Continuous mathematics, on the other hand, is based on the convenience of manual computation using calculus and similar tools. It is internally consistent and rigorous within its own axiomatic system, but it has no instantiation in physics. There was a subtle shift in the last century: some in the mathematical and physical communities argued that since continuous mathematics is so effective, our world is likely indeed continuous. This philosophy implicitly treats continuous mathematics as reality itself—a kind of mathematical ontologism—and is not grounded in the primary principle of aligning with phenomena. To me, this seems somewhat arrogant. In short, these scholars believe that even though no continuous object has been found in reality, reality must still be continuous—which assumes that their continuous mathematical model is complete. This view overlooks the fact that human rationality is inherently limited, and I therefore consider it a form of arrogance.

6. The continuous representation of reality also brings obvious internal inconsistencies. The assumption of infinite approximability leads to divergences in physical formulas—ultraviolet divergences, curvature divergences at singularities, and paradoxes of infinite interactions. These paradoxes directly violate global energy conservation and contradict the first law of thermodynamics, which is a foundational principle of physics. Physicists have therefore had to introduce various measures to patch this bug. Continuous models also give rise to other problems, such as non-locality in heat diffusion equations. This already hints that reality is likely not continuous, yet due to the computational convenience and general accuracy of continuous mathematics in most cases, a considerable proportion of the physics community still believes the world is continuous.

7. The strongest current defense of "reality is continuous" comes from its proponents, who emphasize that if reality were discrete, Lorentz symmetry could not be realized on a discrete network. I personally see a misconception here. For example, randomly disordered crystalline materials can already achieve a very close approximation to isotropy, and the scale gap between atoms and observable dimensions in such materials is far smaller than the gap between the Planck scale and observable scales. No matter how far one looks, random disorder will statistically average out the "tiny asymmetries" in topological adjacency at the Planck scale, making anisotropy undetectable in observation.

**Conclusion:** Whether the world is continuous or discrete remains, in my view, an open question. Neither side can yet reach a definitive conclusion. This issue will only be better understood when nature itself provides more clues.
